# Classroom Challenge: proposed decision set

**Proposal only. Nothing here is implemented, and no dataset change is proposed by this
document.** It sets out which of the 34 decisions a curated 30–35 minute classroom exercise should
carry, which it should leave to the Full Challenge, and the smallest number of program-level splits
that would make the classroom set coherent.

The Full Challenge keeps all 34 decisions and the complete documentation. Classroom Mode is a
curated teaching path through the same dataset, not a simplified replacement for it.

---

## 1. Headline

| | Full Challenge | Classroom Mode (proposed) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Decisions | 34 | **20** |
| Options | 96 | 54 |
| Visible words on the decision cards | 6,864 | **~4,100** |
| Reading at 220 wpm | ~31 min | **~19 min** |
| Plus ~40 s deliberation per decision | ~23 min | ~13 min |
| **Estimated completion** | **~55–60 min** | **~32–35 min** |

The completion estimate is decision-card reading plus a deliberation allowance, and does not count
the home, overview, methodology or glossary pages, which a class may or may not use. The results
page adds roughly three to four minutes to either.

**Twenty is the ceiling, not a preference.** Under the selection rules given — three decisions per
area, three reserve decisions in total, and the four unsupported "more or less" decisions excluded —
twenty is the largest set that can be assembled from the current dataset plus the two splits
recommended in section 5. Section 6 sets out what would have to change to reach 22.

### Provenance of the 54 options

Counting the eighteen existing decisions and projecting the two new ones as enacted + published
proposal, as every program-level split so far has been:

| Class | Options | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Enacted policy | 20 | 37% |
| Published proposal | 15 | 28% |
| Documented alternative | 5 | 9% |
| Illustrative allocation scenario | 14 | 26% |

For comparison the Full Challenge is 35% enacted, 19% proposal, 13% documented and **33%
illustrative**. Classroom Mode shifts the balance toward material somebody actually published, which
is the point of curating it.

Twelve of the fourteen remaining illustrative options are the second, opposite-direction option on a
card that already carries a published proposal. They stay because removing them would leave several
cards offering movement in one direction only, which teaches something false about budgeting. Each
still carries the full caveat on the card, never behind a disclosure.

Computed against the eighteen existing decisions only, so the two new splits can only widen the

| | Amount |

| Starting unappropriated balance | $1,000,000,000 |
| Every decision left at enacted | $1,000,000,000 |

Surplus, near-balance and deficit are all reachable, and zero lies inside the range with room on

while relying on −$1,137,500,000 of one-time actions, which is exactly the confusion section 7 is

## 5. Program-level splits recommended

**Two, and only two.** Both are recommended because they fill a subject-area gap that cannot be
filled any other way, not because the items are large.

### Recommended

**1. UNC System Enrollment Growth Adjustment** — Budget Book p. 89, item 6, $153,495,386 recurring.

*Why it is necessary.* Without it, Classroom Mode contains **no UNC decision at all**. Both existing
UNC decisions are excluded on the rules: one is a vague residual, the other is two unsupported
percentages that no published document can rescue. The UNC system is one of the largest and most
politically contested parts of the state budget, and a classroom exercise that omits it entirely is
not a state budget exercise. This is the only route back in.

*Why this item rather than the larger UNC Compensation Increase Reserve (p. 89, item 1,
$198,248,000).* The set already contains two compensation decisions. A third would be repetitive,
and enrollment-driven funding teaches something no other card teaches: that some appropriations
follow a headcount the legislature does not directly control.

*Before implementing:* the four-condition bridge test, and in particular whether the Committee
Report carries a matching enrolment adjustment for budget code 16011 of the same scope.

**2. Exceptional Children New Funding Formula** — Budget Book p. 73, item 25, $63,450,653 recurring
plus $33,434,785 nonrecurring.

*Why it is necessary.* With the Public Schools aggregate barred, K-12 would otherwise be teacher pay
and Opportunity Scholarships: two decisions about who is paid and where children go to school, and
nothing about how the money reaches classrooms. Special education appears nowhere in the current
34 decisions. This item is a formula change with a named policy behind it, at a scale that matters.

*Before implementing:* the bridge test, and particular care with the nonrecurring component, which
must be carried as a real split rather than converted by convention, exactly as teacher pay was.

| --- | ---: | --- |

| Community college compensation (p. 85, item 2) | $80,350,000 R | Community colleges are already represented, and this would be a third compensation card. Fills nothing. |

Note that splitting is not a general remedy for a vague residual. It shrinks one. Removing the two
largest published items from the Public Schools aggregate would still leave roughly $617m under the
label "the Governor's other recommendations". The Health Benefits card was fixed by *naming every
item*, not by splitting, and that is the pattern the other three residuals should follow in the
Full Challenge.

## 6. What would raise the set to 22

Under the current rules, twenty is the maximum. Each of these adds one:

1. **Resolve the Commerce scope mismatch.** Establishing whether the act's $140,493,128 Commerce
   line corresponds to Budget Book codes 14600, 14601 and 14602 would produce a published proposal
   and restore a programme decision to Economic and Community Development — which is currently
   represented only by a reserve.
2. **Name the contents of the Adult Correction residual**, as the Health Benefits card now does.
   That makes it usable, though Justice would then need a fourth slot or a substitution.
3. **Build the public school personnel bonus split** and raise the K-12 cap to four, pairing it with
   teacher pay as an explicit recurring-versus-one-time contrast.

I would take (1) first: it is the only one that fixes an area with no programme representation at
all, and it is a documents question rather than a design question.

## 7. Showing when a balanced year rests on one-time money

**The problem.** A student can close a deficit by halving a reserve and, in the same session, adopt
several recurring spending increases. The balance reads as solved. The following year, the reserve
reduction is gone and every recurring commitment remains. Nothing in the current results page makes
that visible as a single fact.

**What must not be said.** Not "you have created a structural deficit." The application cannot
calculate one. A structural deficit compares *recurring revenue* against *recurring obligations* for
the whole budget, and this dataset contains neither: it carries the enacted appropriation level, the
availability statement, and the fiscal impact of *changes*. The act's availability statement mixes
recurring tax collections with reversions, transfers and prior-year cash balances, and no source in
this project separates them. Claiming a structural deficit would require inventing a recurring
revenue baseline, which is exactly the kind of manufactured figure this project refuses elsewhere.

**What can be calculated exactly, from data already in the dataset.** Every option already carries
its recurring and nonrecurring components separately, and the engine already sums them:

| Measure | How | Already computed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Net recurring policy change | recurring revenue − recurring spending − recurring reserve, over selected options | Yes — `totals.structuralChange` |
| Net nonrecurring policy change | the same over the nonrecurring components | Yes — `totals.spending.nonrecurring`, `.revenue`, `.reserve` |
| How much of the final balance depends on one-time actions | the nonrecurring component of the total change, against the change as a whole | Derivable, not yet surfaced |
| Which selected options carry the one-time money | the options whose nonrecurring component is non-zero | Derivable, not yet surfaced |

None of that needs a revenue baseline. All of it is arithmetic on figures each option already
carries with a citation.

**Proposed results-page panel.** Shown only when the session's net recurring change and net
nonrecurring change point in opposite directions — the condition that actually matters — and
phrased as a comparison rather than a verdict:

> **This year's balance and next year's position**
>
> Your recurring changes leave the state committed to **$X more each year** than the enacted budget.
> Your one-time changes provide **$Y in this year only**. Of the $Z you moved, **$Y came from
> actions that do not repeat**: [named options].
>
> This says nothing about whether the state's finances are in balance over the longer run. That
> would require comparing recurring revenue with recurring obligations across the whole budget, and
> this exercise does not carry the figures to do it. What it does show is that a balance reached
> partly with one-time money starts the next year with the recurring commitments still in place.

The same three lines belong in the CSV, the JSON and the printed report, and the first two are
already exported.

**One caution about the current design.** The two largest one-time levers in the dataset —
Stabilization and Inflation at $1,325,485,236 and SCIF at $1,139,200,000 — are both excluded from
Classroom Mode, which means the classroom set makes this lesson *harder* to stumble into, not
easier. Whether that is right depends on the intent. If the point is to teach the danger, one large
reserve lever should probably be kept in and the panel above built alongside it. I would keep the
current exclusions and build the panel first, then reconsider with the panel in place.

## 8. Open questions for review

1. **Twenty rather than 22.** Accept twenty, or resolve the Commerce mismatch first to reach 21?
2. **Both splits, or only UNC enrollment growth?** UNC is the one that is strictly necessary.
   Exceptional Children is a quality upgrade that displaces the residential schools card.
3. **Should one large reserve lever stay in**, so the one-time-money lesson can actually be
   triggered?
4. **Is the four-condition bridge test to be run on both split candidates before or after** the
   Classroom Mode selection is frozen? Either order works; running it first risks discovering that a
   candidate does not bridge, which would change the selection.

Nothing in this document has been implemented. The baseline, the reconciliation ledger, both
section 26.10 readings, every decision amount and the full 34-decision challenge are untouched.
